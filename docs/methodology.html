Our models

Beat the Streak

Each hitter's hit rates are blended over the season and his last 81, 30 and 10 days (recent games count more), pulled toward the league for small samples, and combined with today's matchup - the opposing starter's and bullpen's PAVE and the park - in a model trained on past seasons and calibrated so its percentages mean what they say. A hitter qualifies only if both his hit chance and his games-with-a-hit rate clear 70%; the best Approach is the pick. Whether to risk the streak today is an optimal-stopping decision (see Optimal stopping).

MLB game picks

Each team's Pythagorean strength (from runs scored and allowed), its schedule-adjusted rating and its hitting power, adjusted by the starter and bullpen it faces today and home field; then calibrated. Every pick is logged before the game and graded after, next to Vegas's closing line. A bet is suggested only when our chance beats the de-vigged market by enough, sized by fractional Kelly in units.

NFL game picks

A logistic regression on each team's Pythagorean strength, schedule-adjusted confidence, EPA per play on offense and allowed on defense (per play, so a defense isn't judged by how many snaps it faced), turnover margin and points per drive, plus each starting QB's QB+. The starter comes from the schedule, checked against the latest roster: one on injured reserve or otherwise inactive (who won't be on the injury report) is replaced by whoever has been starting. Trained on every game since 2016 with each week predicted only from earlier games, the feature set chosen by walk-forward validation and checked on a season it never saw. When we disagree sharply with Vegas we defer to it (it's proven right more often there), then calibrate.

Injury report and What if?

A team's run game, pass game, run defense and pass defense are rated in percent of their normal yards per play. A player out lowers his units by what an absence at his position has cost teams in results (or what Vegas charges, where results are too noisy), times his snap share; a QB change goes through the model with the new QB's rating. Yards become points (0.07 a yard) and points become win chance (0.137 log-odds a point, from how Vegas turns spreads into odds). The final injury report's Out and Doubtful players reprice each game the morning of, or the day before, it.

Stuff+

A grade for every pitch from how it's thrown, never where it went or what happened: velocity, movement, spin and spin axis, release height, side and extension, arm angle, approach angle at the plate, and its difference in speed and movement from the pitcher's own fastball. A gradient-boosted model per pitch group (fastballs, breaking balls, off-speed) learns what pitches like it are worth in runs - with balls in play valued by their exit velocity and launch angle, not by where the fielders stood. Each pitcher is graded by a model that never saw him, so it can't simply remember his results. 100 is league average, every 10 points one standard deviation between pitchers, pulled toward 100 for small samples. It holds up: a pitcher's grade in odd games matches his grade in even games at r = 0.91 (his actual runs allowed, 0.24), and 2025 Stuff+ adds to what his 2025 results say about 2026.

Decision+

A grade for a hitter's swing-or-take choices. Every pitch he saw is placed by where it crossed his own strike zone, the count, the outs, the runners, the pitch type and the pitcher's hand. League models give, for that spot, how often hitters swing and what swinging and taking are each worth in runs - league-average results, so it's the decision being graded, not his contact. Each pitch scores what his choice was worth over the league's usual mix there. 100 is average, every 10 points one standard deviation between hitters. It's one of the steadiest things a hitter does (r = 0.75 from 2025 to 2026, more than walk rate's 0.61), it predicts next season's walk rate (r = 0.48) and adds to it, and it says nothing about strikeouts - whether he makes contact is a separate skill.

MLB game simulation

Every pitch is drawn from the stored Statcast pitches of the last two seasons. The pitcher picks a pitch as he does in that count against that side of the plate, at his speed for it. Its result - a ball, called strike, swinging strike, foul, ball in play or hit batter - is the league's rate for that pitch type in that count, times the pitcher's own rate for that pitch, the hitter's against everything he's seen (each measured against what the league would have done with the same pitches in the same counts, and, for small samples, started from what his Stuff+ or Decision+ says until a few hundred pitches of his own take over), the platoon edge and the times through the order. A ball in play becomes a groundout, flyout, lineout, single, double, triple or home run the same way, and the runners move as they did after that kind of play with those runners on and that many outs - double plays, sacrifice flies and taking the extra base included. Starters go about as many pitches as they have lately; relievers come in by role, the closer for a save. Between pitches runners steal, get caught or picked off and move up on wild pitches as often as they do in that base-out spot, and hitters are walked intentionally as often as managers call it there. Extra innings start with a runner on second. Run over many games it scores about 4.4 runs a team, strikes out 23% of hitters, walks 9.9% and steals 0.74 bases a game, against the league's 4.5, 22%, 10% and 0.74. "Simulate it 1,000 times" plays the matchup a thousand times: who wins how often, the spread of margins and the commonest scores, and each player's averages, next to our pick model's chance for the same game.

Game simulation

Every snap is drawn from the teams' own play-by-play this season: how often each throws on early downs, how its runs and completions are spread, and how often it's sacked, picked off or fumbles, pulled toward the league's with 150 plays of league average. Field position matters the way it does for the league: inside the 10 teams run more, complete fewer passes and gain less (real teams complete 49% there against 66% between the 20s), and on 4th and 2 there they go for it 61% of the time; pinned inside their own 10 they run more and rarely lose yards into a safety. A team's own red-zone numbers aren't used - they barely carry over from one half of a season to the next. Kickoffs, punts, field goals by distance and extra points use the league's last two seasons. The profiles carry style, not strength: each team's gains are scaled to the league's average, and the game is then tuned, from 300 seeded simulations, so the average simulated game lands on the What if? panel's win chance (as a point margin, 0.137 log-odds a point) and our predicted total. Unit settings move the yards where they belong - a pass defense at 80% gives up more through the air. Play calls follow how teams call them: short yardage mostly runs, and to the lead back (late in a close game too); a team ahead late runs the clock, throwing about as rarely as real ones; fourth downs go for it the way teams do now (4th and 1 near midfield, 4th and short when behind late); a team kneels only when kneeling on every down left runs out the clock, timeouts and the two-minute warning counted. Overtime gives both teams the ball. Penalties come at each team's rate a snap (pulled hard toward the league's - a team's flag rate barely carries over from one half of a season to the next) in the league's mix: false starts and holding walked back, pass interference spotted, roughing and other personal fouls added on with a first down. Kickoffs and punts are real ones from the last two seasons: a kickoff is a touchback, out of bounds, or caught at a spot and returned (a deep catch comes back farther), and a punt kicked from about where the offense is, then returned, fair caught, downed, out of bounds or a touchback - with return touchdowns as often as real ones. Onside kicks are left out (half of the real kickoff drives that start past midfield followed one); a team behind in the 4th quarter kicks onside, and recovers about 1 in 13. Each team gets three timeouts a half; it spends them as teams do - the defense to stop the clock late when it's within two scores, the offense when it's behind late or driving at the half - and the two-minute warning stops the clock. Each team's targets and carries go to its players by their shares over its last four games, among those still active; a player's catch rate and gains are his own next to his teammates', pulled toward theirs. Mark a player out in What if? and his share goes to the rest; swap the QB and the new one throws and takes the QB's runs. Simulate it 1,000 times plays the same game a thousand times for how often each side wins, the spread of margins and totals, and each player's averages - how often he scores, and how often he reaches 100 yards.

NFL team units

Each unit's EPA per play, solved for every offense and defense at once so each is credited for the opponents it faced, with recent games weighted more and reaching back into last season. Shown on a 100 = league-average scale, 30 points per standard deviation.

Player props, age curves, prospects

Props: a player's production blended over his season, last 8 and last 4 games, against what his opponent allows his position. Age curves: nearest neighbors among historical seasons at the same age. Prospects: each source's ranks blended into one list. The Stats pages (WAVE, CAVE, WOPS, QB+ and the rest) describe; only the Picks pages predict.

Site terms

Beat the Streak

MLB's contest: pick a hitter each day; the streak grows when he gets a hit and resets when he doesn't. 57 in a row wins the grand prize - one more than Joe DiMaggio's record.

Injury report: before / after

An NFL pick logged Tuesday, before the final injury report (purple outline), or repriced with it the morning of, or the day before, the game.

Regular

A player on at least half his side's snaps over his last four active games. What if? lists anyone at 30%.

Average regular / average starter

In What if?, a typical regular at a position (or a typical starting QB) - for someone not listed, or not yet rated.

Unit %

In What if?, a unit's yards per play as a percent of its normal; a defense at 90% allows 10% more.

Hitting

WAVE

A hitter's batting average - hits per at-bat - blended over the full season and his last 81, 30 and 10 days, so recent games count more, and pulled toward the league average when he has few at-bats (see shrinkage). Walks don't count as at-bats, so they neither help nor hurt it.

On the site: MLB Stats, and underneath every Beat the Streak pick.

Hit chance

His chance of at least one hit in a typical game of 3.5 at-bats at his WAVE: one minus the chance he misses every time, 1 - (1 - WAVE)^3.5. A .280 hitter gets about 68% (see the complement rule).

On the site: MLB Stats.

CAVE (Carried Average)

The share of a hitter's games with at least one hit. A 1-for-4 and a 4-for-4 count the same - it measures how steadily he hits, not how much.

On the site: MLB Stats.

OPS and WOPS

OPS is on-base percentage (how often he reaches base) plus slugging (total bases per at-bat), a quick measure of overall hitting. WOPS is his OPS blended over the same windows as WAVE.

On the site: MLB Stats.

vs. pitchers faced

A hitter's numbers next to what a league-average hitter would have done against the same pitchers. Two hitters can both hit .270; the one who did it against aces did more.

On the site: the MLB Stats hitter table's second view.

Approach

A hitter's games-with-a-hit rate times his hit chance - how Beat the Streak ranks the hitters who qualify (both above 70%).

On the site: MLB Picks.

Decision+

A grade for a hitter's swing-or-take choices: each pitch he saw is placed by where it crossed his strike zone, the count, outs, runners and pitch type, and scored by what his choice was worth over what the league usually does there. 100 is average; every 10 points is one standard deviation. It grades the decision, not the contact.

On the site: MLB Stats; it also starts the simulator's estimate for hitters with few pitches.

wOBA and xwOBA

wOBA weights each way of reaching base by the runs it's worth - a home run counts about twice a single, not four times. xwOBA is the wOBA a batted ball "should" have produced from its exit velocity and launch angle, whether or not a fielder made a great play.

On the site: Stuff+ values balls in play the same way, by exit velocity and launch angle.

Pitching

PAVE and PAVE+

PAVE is the batting average hitters manage against a pitcher, blended over the same windows as WAVE. PAVE+ compares it with the league: below 1 is tougher than average.

On the site: MLB Stats (today's starters, pitchers giving up the most hits).

Stuff+

A grade for how a pitch is thrown - velocity, movement, spin, release, approach angle and how it plays off the pitcher's fastball - never where it went or what happened. 100 is league average; every 10 points is one standard deviation between pitchers, so a 120 is about two above.

On the site: MLB Stats (Best stuff), and the simulator.

FIP (Fielding Independent Pitching)

An ERA-like number built only from what a pitcher controls without his fielders: home runs, walks and hit batters, and strikeouts - (13 HR + 3 (BB + HBP) - 2 K) / IP plus a constant that puts it on ERA's scale.

On the site: Age Curves' pitcher projections.

Platoon edge

Hitters do better against pitchers who throw from the other side - a lefty against a righty sees the ball longer. The simulator applies the league's measured edge to every pitch.

On the site: the MLB game simulation.

Times through the order

Hitters do better each time they see the same pitcher in a game. It's one reason starters come out around the third time through.

On the site: the MLB game simulation.

Teams and games

Run differential

Runs scored minus runs allowed. Over a season it says more about a team than its record, which close games and luck push around.

On the site: MLB Stats (team schedules and profiles).

Pythagorean strength

The winning percentage a team's runs (or points) scored and allowed predict: RS^x / (RS^x + RA^x). With baseball's exponent of 1.83, a team scoring 750 and allowing 650 "should" win about 56.5%, or 92 games. Bill James named it for its resemblance to the Pythagorean theorem.

On the site: both game-pick models and the team profiles.

Strength of schedule

How good a team's opponents have been. A 10-5 start against the league's best is worth more than one against its worst.

On the site: team profiles, and the pick models' schedule-adjusted ratings.

Park factor

How much a ballpark adds to or takes away from scoring compared with an average park - each park's runs a game over the league's. Coors Field's thin air is the famous high one.

On the site: team profiles, and the hit chances in Beat the Streak.

Overall rating and recent form

Our blend of a team's strength measures into one number (1.00 is average), and the same over its recent games.

On the site: MLB Stats (Teams).

Football

EPA (expected points added)

Every down, distance and spot on the field is worth some number of points on average to the team with the ball - 1st and 10 at the opponent's 20 is worth far more than at your own 20. A play's EPA is how much it changed that. A 3rd-and-8 completion for 6 yards gains yards but loses expected points.

On the site: NFL Stats and Picks.

Net EPA per play

A team's offensive EPA per play minus the EPA per play its defense allows - one number for how much better it is than its opponents, play by play.

On the site: NFL power rankings.

QB+

A quarterback's EPA per dropback over his last 16 games, pulled toward a replacement-level backup when he has few.

On the site: NFL game picks, What if? and the Stats tab.

Receiving+, Rushing+, Pass D+, Run D+

Team unit ratings from EPA per play, each credited for the opponents it faced. 100 is league average, higher is always stronger, and 30 points is one standard deviation.

On the site: NFL Stats (Team units).

Points per drive and turnover margin

Points a team scores per possession, and takeaways minus giveaways. Both feed the NFL pick model.

On the site: NFL game picks.

Snap share

The share of his side's plays a player was on the field for. A regular is on at least half over his last four active games.

On the site: What if? and Player Props.

Floor

The fewest a player has put up in a single game this season - his worst day.

On the site: NFL Player Props.

Payroll

Relative payroll

A team's payroll per game over the league's that season. 2.0 spent twice the average. Dividing by the league's lets 1995 and 2025 be compared without adjusting for inflation.

On the site: Payroll.

Payroll effectiveness

Wins over average divided by payroll per win over the league's. Above 1 bought more winning per dollar than the league did.

On the site: Payroll.

Expected wins

What the ten seasons whose relative payroll was closest won, plus the front office's recent edge - what a team "should" win for what it spent.

On the site: Payroll.

Front office score

How many wins a franchise beat its payroll's expectation by, averaged over its previous three seasons.

On the site: Payroll.

Spending change

A team's relative payroll minus its average over the previous three seasons - positive means it spent up.

On the site: Payroll.

Cash paid and cap spending

For NFL teams: the dollars actually paid to players that season (signing bonuses counted when paid), or what counted against the salary cap (bonuses spread over the contract).

On the site: NFL Payroll.

Betting

Moneyline

American odds on who wins. -150 means you risk $150 to win $100; +130 means $100 wins $130.

On the site: the "Vegas" number on every game pick.

Implied probability

The chance a price implies: -150 is 150 / 250 = 60%; +130 is 100 / 230 = 43.5%. Turning odds into a probability is converting odds.

On the site: every game pick's Vegas number.

Vig and de-vig

The two sides' implied chances add to more than 100% - 60% + 43.5% = 103.5% above. The extra is the sportsbook's cut, the vig. De-vigging scales both down to add to 100% (58% and 42%), the book's real view.

On the site: "Vegas" always means the de-vigged chance.

Closing line

The odds just before the game starts, after the market has absorbed all the news. Beating it is the hardest test of a pick model.

On the site: every MLB pick is graded against it.

Edge

Our chance minus the market's. We suggest a bet only when it's at least 5 points.

On the site: MLB Picks.

Units

A standard stake - 1% of a bankroll - so records compare across bettors. Sizes come from the Kelly rule.

On the site: MLB Picks' bet record.

1. Describing data

Before predicting anything, you summarize what happened.

Rates and denominators

The idea A rate is a count over the chances it had - and which chances you count changes what it means.

In sports Batting average divides hits by at-bats; on-base percentage divides by plate appearances, so walks count. A patient hitter looks better by the second.

On this site WAVE counts at-bats only. We tested both: replaying the 2026 season, the Beat the Streak picks hit more often using at-bats than every plate appearance.

Learn more →

Weighted averages

The idea An average where some numbers count more than others.

In sports Is a hitter's last week or his whole season the better guide? Some of both: recent games say more about his form now, the season says more about who he is.

On this site WAVE, CAVE and WOPS weight the full season and the last 81, 30 and 10 days, so a hot or cold stretch moves them without taking over.

Learn more →

Standard deviation

The idea How spread out a set of numbers is - roughly, how far a typical value sits from the average.

In sports Two teams both average 4.5 runs; one scores 4 or 5 almost every night, the other 0 or 9. Same average, very different standard deviation - and the steady one wins more close games.

On this site It's the unit for our "plus" grades: on Stuff+ and Decision+ every 10 points is one standard deviation, on the NFL unit ratings every 30.

Learn more →

Z-scores and "plus" scales

The idea A z-score is how many standard deviations a value is from the average: (value - average) / standard deviation. It puts numbers with different units on one scale.

In sports Is a 98 mph fastball or a 3,000 rpm curveball more special? Their units can't be compared; their z-scores can.

On this site Stuff+ is 100 + 10 × z. A 120 is two standard deviations above average - in a bell curve, about the top 2-3% of pitchers.

Learn more →

Percentiles

The idea The share of a group a value is better than. The 90th percentile beats 90%.

In sports Baseball Savant's red-and-blue bars are percentiles: a 99 in exit velocity hits the ball harder than 99% of hitters.

On this site The bars in Look up a player rank him among regular players, worst on the left to best on the right.

Learn more →

2. Small samples

Sports hand you small samples every day. Knowing how little they say is half of analytics.

Sample size and noise

The idea The fewer the chances, the more luck moves a rate.

In sports A true .250 hitter goes 4-for-5 or better about 1.6% of the time - rare in any one game, but with about 270 hitters starting every day, someone does it almost daily. Nobody thinks he became an .800 hitter.

On this site It's why every rate we show is blended or pulled toward the league, and why the picks pages show how many picks a record is from.

Learn more →

Regression to the mean

The idea An extreme result is usually part skill, part luck. The luck doesn't repeat, so the next result tends to land closer to average.

In sports The "Sports Illustrated cover jinx": players reach the cover after a career-best stretch, and the next stretch is usually worse - not a curse, just luck running out.

On this site Decision+ carries over from one season to the next at a correlation of 0.75, so a hitter 20 points above average is expected about 15 above next year. NFL penalty rates barely carry over from one half of a season to the next, so the simulator pulls every team's nearly all the way to the league's.

Learn more →

Shrinkage

The idea Estimate a rate as if the player started with some extra, average results already on his record. With few real results the average dominates; with many, his own do. (It's a simple form of Bayesian estimation.)

In sports Add 50 at-bats of a .245 league average to a hitter's line: a 4-for-5 start becomes (4 + 12.25) / (5 + 50) = .295, while 60-for-200 barely moves (.289).

On this site That's WAVE's exact recipe. Stuff+ adds 100 average pitches, Decision+ 300, NFL team rates 150 plays - and the simulator starts a pitcher or hitter with few pitches from what his Stuff+ or Decision+ says.

Learn more →

Confidence intervals

The idea A range of values the truth could plausibly be, given the data - wider when the sample is small.

In sports A pick model that's 7-for-10 might be a 70% model - or a 40% one that got lucky. The 95% interval for 7 of 10 runs from about 40% to 89%. At 70 of 100 it narrows to 60% to 78%.

On this site Our backtests report intervals, not just averages - see the bootstrap below.

3. Probability

The language every pick on this site is written in.

The complement rule

The idea The chance something happens at least once is one minus the chance it never does.

In sports A .280 hitter misses 72% of the time, so in 3.5 at-bats he misses every time about 0.72^3.5 = 32% of the time - and gets at least one hit 68%.

On this site That's hit chance.

Learn more →

Independence

The idea Two events are independent if one happening doesn't change the chance of the other. Then you can multiply their chances.

In sports Two hitters in the same game aren't fully independent: a dominant starter or a cold, windy night hurts them both, so they tend to go hitless together.

On this site Hit chance treats each at-bat as independent - close, but not exact, which is one reason the pick models' chances are checked against real results (see calibration).

Learn more →

Odds and log-odds

The idea Odds are the chance something happens over the chance it doesn't: 60% is 60/40 = 1.5. Log-odds, the logarithm of that, turn a 0-to-100% scale into one with no ends, where adding works.

In sports Each point of NFL spread is worth about 0.137 in log-odds: a 3-point favorite wins about 60%, a 7-point favorite about 72%.

On this site What if? and the NFL simulator turn point edges into win chances this way; a logistic regression works in log-odds throughout.

Learn more →

log5

The idea Bill James' rule for combining two rates measured against everyone into the rate for one matchup, each relative to the league.

In sports A .300 hitter against a pitcher who allows .220, in a .250 league: (.300 × .220 / .250) / (that + .700 × .780 / .750) = .266. Good hitter, tough pitcher - a bit above average.

On this site Beat the Streak's matchup between each hitter's WAVE and the starter's PAVE.

Learn more →

Expected value

The idea The average result of a bet if you could make it over and over: each outcome times its chance, added up.

In sports $100 on a +150 underdog you think wins 45%: 0.45 × $150 - 0.55 × $100 = +$12.50. You'll lose this bet more often than not, and it's still a good one.

On this site A bet is suggested only when our chance gives it a clear positive expected value against the market (an edge of 5 points or more).

Learn more →

4. Relationships and models

How one thing moves with another - and how to turn that into a prediction.

Correlation

The idea A number from -1 to 1 for how closely two things move together in a straight line. 0 is no straight-line relationship; it isn't proof that one causes the other.

In sports Split each pitcher's season into odd and even games. His Stuff+ in one half matches the other at 0.91; his runs allowed, only 0.24. Stuff is a skill you can measure quickly; runs allowed take far longer to mean much.

On this site That split-half test is how we checked Stuff+ (and Decision+, 0.75 season to season) before using either.

Learn more →

Linear regression

The idea The straight line that best fits one number from others. Its slope says how much the result moves per unit of the input.

In sports Across NFL games, every extra yard a team gains is worth about 0.07 points - so a 100-yard edge is about a touchdown.

On this site What if? uses that slope to turn yards into points.

Learn more →

Logistic regression

The idea Regression for yes-or-no outcomes: a weighted sum of inputs, in log-odds, turned into a chance between 0 and 100%.

In sports Win or lose: each input - point differential, the quarterback, turnovers - pushes the log-odds up or down by its weight.

On this site The NFL game picks are a logistic regression on Pythagorean strength, EPA edges, turnover margin, points per drive and each starter's QB+.

Learn more →

Gradient boosting

The idea Hundreds of small decision trees ("is velocity over 95? is the count 0-2?"), each built to fix the mistakes of the ones before. It finds curves and combinations a straight line misses.

In sports A 92 mph fastball can be great with lots of rise and ordinary without - the value of speed depends on movement. Trees catch interactions like that.

On this site Stuff+ is a boosted model per pitch group, and so is the Beat the Streak hit model.

Learn more →

Nearest neighbors

The idea Predict by finding the most similar past cases and seeing what happened to them. No equation - just comparisons.

In sports A 27-year-old with a .280 average: what did the 25 most similar 27-year-old seasons do the next year?

On this site Age Curves projects next season from exactly those 25 comparable seasons, with the range they spanned.

Overfitting

5. Testing ideas

How we decide whether something is real - and whether it's worth using.

Hypothesis tests and p-values

The idea Assume there's no effect, then ask how likely data this extreme would be. That's the p-value. A small one (under 0.05, by convention) says "probably not just chance."

In sports A coin-flip picker goes 60-for-100 about 3% of the time - so a 60% record over 100 picks is decent evidence of skill. Over 10 picks (6-4), it's no evidence at all.

On this site Across 48,804 hitter-games, facing a pitcher with better Stuff+ meant fewer hits at p = 0.00000005 - about as sure as statistics gets.

Learn more →

Significant isn't the same as useful

The idea A p-value says an effect is real, not that it's big - or that it adds anything to what you already know.

In sports Teams do score slightly more in warm weather - but if you already know the park and the pitchers, temperature may add almost nothing.

On this site That Stuff+ effect, real as it was, didn't improve the hit model: it already knew how hittable each pitcher had been, and its accuracy didn't measurably change. So it isn't in the model. (Stuff+ still helps the simulator with pitchers it has seen little of.)

Backtests and holdouts

The idea Test a model on past games it never learned from, scored exactly as it would have been live. A holdout is a final stretch kept untouched until the very end.

In sports It's a mock draft graded against how the players actually turned out.

On this site No change to a pick model ships without one, and the README keeps the results - the ones that failed too.

Walk-forward testing

The idea Train on everything before a date, predict the next stretch, step forward and repeat - never using the future to predict the past.

In sports Predicting May's games with a model trained through April, then June's with one trained through May - the way you'd really have had to do it.

On this site How every pick model is chosen and checked, by month or by week.

Data leakage

The idea When a model sees information it wouldn't have had at prediction time. Its backtest looks great; live, it falls apart.

In sports Rating a starter with a season ERA that includes the very game you're predicting.

On this site Every feature is computed through the day before the game - a pitcher's Stuff+ in a backtest uses only pitches he'd already thrown.

The bootstrap

The idea To see how much a result could vary, resample your own data - draw from it, with replacement, a thousand times - and recompute it each time. The spread is your uncertainty.

In sports Rebuild a season by drawing 162 games at random from the real ones, a thousand times, and see how much the team's record swings.

On this site Our model comparisons report a bootstrapped 95% interval for the difference - for the Stuff+ test, -0.0007 to +0.0005 in Brier score, which straddles zero: no measurable change.

6. Judging forecasts

A 60% pick that loses wasn't necessarily wrong. These tools grade probabilities fairly.

Brier score

The idea Score each forecast by how far it was from what happened, counting a win as 1 and a loss as 0: (forecast - result)². Squaring makes a big miss cost much more than a small one. Average over every game; lower is better, and 0 is perfect.

In sports You give a team a 70% chance. If it wins, the result is 1: (0.70 - 1)² = 0.09. If it loses, the result is 0: (0.70 - 0)² = 0.49. Saying 50% scores (0.50 - 1)² or (0.50 - 0)² = 0.25 either way, so 0.25 is the coin-flip baseline. If your 70% teams really win 7 in 10, you average 0.7 × 0.09 + 0.3 × 0.49 = 0.21 - better than the coin flip.

On this site Baseball games are close to coin flips - our MLB game models land around 0.244, a real but modest edge on 0.25. It's the score we choose models by.

Log loss

The idea Like Brier, but a confident miss costs far more: saying 99% and being wrong is a disaster.

In sports "There's no way they lose" - log loss is the score that makes that expensive.

Calibration

The idea Forecasts are calibrated when they mean what they say: the games called 60% are won about 60% of the time.

In sports A weather forecaster's 30% days should rain about 3 times in 10. A picker whose "locks" win 55% isn't calibrated.

On this site The MLB Picks chart "When we were more sure, were we right more often?" is a calibration check, and every model's chances are adjusted after training so they line up.

AUC

The idea How well a model ranks: the chance it puts a random "yes" above a random "no". 0.5 is a coin flip, 1.0 is perfect. It ignores whether the percentages themselves are right.

In sports Does the hitter we ranked higher get a hit more often than the one we ranked lower?

On this site The Beat the Streak hit model's is about 0.60. That sounds low - but a single game is mostly luck, which is exactly why a long streak is so hard.

In sports Betting markets pool thousands of bettors' money and information; their closing lines are hard to beat.

On this site Every pick sits next to the de-vigged Vegas chance, and when the NFL model disagrees with Vegas sharply, it defers - Vegas has been right more often there.

7. Simulation

When the math gets too tangled to solve, play it out.

Monte Carlo simulation

The idea Answer "how often?" by playing a random process many times and counting. The more runs, the smaller the error.

In sports Play a matchup 1,000 times; if one team wins 600, its chance is about 60% - give or take 3 points, since 2 × √(0.6 × 0.4 / 1,000) ≈ 0.03.

On this site "Simulate it 1,000 times" in both game simulators: win chances, the spread of scores and each player's chances of a hit, a homer or 100 yards.

Markov chains and base-out states

The idea A process that moves between states, where what happens next depends only on where you are now, not how you got there.

In sports Baseball has 24 base-out states (8 ways to fill the bases, 3 out counts). A single with a runner on first and one out leads to first-and-second 72% of the time and first-and-third 25%.

On this site The MLB simulator moves runners exactly this way, with each play's real outcomes in each state; football's equivalent is down, distance and field position.

Checking a simulation against reality

The idea A simulation is only as good as its match to real games, so measure it.

In sports If simulated teams score 5.5 runs a game, something's wrong - real ones score about 4.5.

On this site Over 2,000 simulated games, MLB teams score 4.44 runs, strike out 22.9% and steal 0.74 bases a game, against the league's 4.46, 22.2% and 0.74.

8. Making decisions

A good forecast still needs a good decision rule.

The Kelly rule

The idea How much of a bankroll to stake on a bet with an edge: enough to grow it fastest over time, never so much that a losing run ruins you. Stake more when the edge is bigger.

In sports At even money with a 55% chance, Kelly says 10% of the bankroll. Since our 55% is itself an estimate, we bet half that.

On this site Suggested bets use half-Kelly in units, capped at 2 units a game and 5 a day.

Optimal stopping

The idea When to stop and lock in what you have versus keep going, solved by working backward from the end (dynamic programming).

In sports In Beat the Streak, a long streak is worth protecting: some days the best pick isn't good enough to risk it, and sitting out keeps the streak alive.

On this site Each day's play-or-sit call compares the pick's chance with a threshold that depends on the current streak and the days left - the "Sat out" days in the Picks log.
