What each tool does

Today

One page for the day: Beat the Streak picks (on days there are any), today's baseball game picks and this week's football picks, each next to how our picks have done.

MLB · Picks

Beat the Streak: the hitters most likely to get a hit today, for MLB's contest of picking a hitter a day and building a streak - with our own running streak. Hit chances: every hitter's chance of a hit today. Game picks: who we think wins each game, next to Vegas, with our record and any bet we'd suggest.

MLB · Stats

What feeds the picks: every hitter's WAVE, hit chance, CAVE and WOPS (as played, or adjusted for the pitchers he faced), active hit streaks, a player lookup, today's probable starters, team ratings and each team's schedule. The schedule also shows what one unit a game would have made betting on that team's opponent to win, and on the team itself, at closing odds, with the last 10 games on their own beside the season total.

MLB · Age Curves

How a player's season compares with history at his age: the 25 most similar seasons by players the same age, and what those players did the next year - his projection.

MLB · Prospects

Several published prospect and draft rankings blended into one consensus list.

MLB · Payroll

Which teams get the most winning for their money, every season since 1988 (opening-day payrolls from The Baseball Cube): payroll effectiveness, expected wins from payroll (what the 10 seasons that spent most like it won, plus the front office's recent edge), wins against that, all-time franchise averages and year-by-year grids, with the whole thing downloadable as a workbook.

MLB · Step In

A game: bat against any pitcher from last season. His pitches, their speeds and how often he throws each one in each count are real; how a pitch of each kind moves is typical for the kind; and what your swing does is made up for the game. It is not one of our models and nothing else on the site uses it.

NFL · Picks

The page leads with the model's season numbers, then a matchup picker: choose a game (or tap one of the week's bets under it) and everything below is about that game. In order: the pick next to Vegas, outlined by whether it came before the final injury report (Tuesday) or after it (the morning of, or the day before, the game), with the ranges the margin and the point total land in half the time and 4 times in 5 (how); the units matchup, each side's passing and rushing against the other's defense, with both teams' unit ratings; the last five meetings at this home team's field (back to 1999, neutral-field games left out) with the Vegas line, over/under and final score; What if? (Deep dive): swap a starting QB, mark players out or set any unit's efficiency and see the chance move, or replay last week's games to test your settings against how they finished; Simulate this game, snap by snap over about five minutes with a drive chart, a final box score and every player's stat line, or 1,000 times at once; and the bets on this game.

Bets: the only bets we recommend are ones that have beaten the closing line in testing. There are two kinds. Over/unders where our total sits 5 or more points from Vegas's (58.6% of 174 past bets at the real odds, tested without knowing whether it rained; 52.4% breaks even). And spread sides where our margin sits 3.5 or more points from Vegas's: 57.3% of 848 against the closing spread over 2002–2026, a winning record in 19 of 25 seasons, though only 35–36 over the last four. Each bet shows the worst line and price still worth taking. A spread bet on an underdog getting 6.5 points or fewer also shows the moneyline as another way to play it: those points rarely decide the game, and on bets like it the moneyline has returned a little more while winning less often. On an underdog getting more, the note says to stay with the points. Our game winners on their own have not beaten the closing line, so they are shown as picks, not bets.

Below the game: the track record (our confidence against Vegas's, with the record in each cell); How strong is the evidence?, which tests each kind of bet in a way that stays honest when checked every week (how); and recent results, folded closed until you open it.

Places to look, not checked against sportsbook lines.

Our models

Beat the Streak

Each hitter's hit rates are blended over the season and his last 81, 30 and 10 days (recent games count more), pulled toward the league for small samples, and combined with today's matchup - the opposing starter's and bullpen's PAVE and the park - in a model trained on past seasons and calibrated so its percentages mean what they say. A hitter qualifies only if both his hit chance and his games-with-a-hit rate clear 70%; the best Approach is the pick. Whether to risk the streak today is an optimal-stopping decision (see Optimal stopping).

MLB game picks

Each team's Pythagorean strength (from runs scored and allowed), its schedule-adjusted rating and its hitting power, adjusted by the starter and bullpen it faces today and home field; then calibrated. Every pick is logged before the game and graded after, next to Vegas's closing line. A bet is suggested only when our chance beats the de-vigged market by enough, sized by fractional Kelly in units.

NFL game picks

A team rating that knows who is playing quarterback and listens to the betting line. Each team's rating moves with every result (more for a big win, a third of the way back to average each offseason). Every quarterback has his own running rating from his box scores (yards, touchdowns and completions for; interceptions and sacks against; his rushing too), measured against the defenses he faced, and every team has one for whoever has been playing the position. A starter above his team's usual level lifts its chance and a backup lowers it, so the team rating means "this team with its normal quarterback play". After each game the two teams' ratings also move part of the way toward what that game's closing point spread said about them, because a past line knows things a final score doesn't, such as who was hurt. The line for the game being picked is never used.

Three smaller things came from replaying the ratings over 25 seasons and asking what their misses had in common. Rest: each extra day off over the opponent, a bye or a Thursday game behind it, is worth a little. Backups: a quarterback standing in for the season's regular starter plays about a point worse than his own record says, because that record was earned in other seasons and other roles. And resting starters: when a team with ten or more wins sits its quarterback in the last week of the season, the rest of its starters are sitting too. The ratings used to give those teams a 56% chance; they won 32%. Together these made the model more accurate in 21 of the 25 seasons. We tried about thirty other ideas the same way (travel, time zones, division games, cold-weather games for dome teams, turnover luck, new coaches, night games) and none held up.

These are the inputs of the best public model we could find, rebuilt from our own data, and they replaced our earlier model outright: in a replay of 1,999 games since 2019, with only what was known before each one, the new model was more accurate in every one of the eight seasons, closed about two thirds of our gap to Vegas, and did better than that public model on its own games. Vegas is still more accurate than we are. The starter comes from the schedule, checked against the latest roster: one on injured reserve or otherwise inactive (who won't be on the injury report) is replaced by whoever has been starting. The ratings run over every game since 2016, and each game is picked only from what came before it. When we disagree sharply with Vegas we defer to it (it's proven right more often there), then calibrate.

The same ratings give a margin in points, and that is what we set against the point spread. Replayed over 6,636 games since 2002 (17 of those seasons were never looked at when the model was built), the bigger our disagreement with the closing spread, the more often our side covered: 51.5% over every game, 55.8% at 3 or more points apart, 57.3% of 848 at 3.5 or more. We checked that it isn't simply betting underdogs (every underdog, blindly, covered 51.0% and lost money), that it isn't late quarterback news (it holds with and without a quarterback change), and that it doesn't hinge on one setting. The honest caveats: a replay flatters, nearby settings land between 55% and 58%, and the last four seasons went 35–36, so the market may have caught up. We mark those sides as bets, say so, and grade every one.

Each game's card adds what a number can't: the case for the pick and what cuts against it (a gap of six or more places in offense, defense or quarterback rank counts as an edge), who the final injury report took out, and how picks like it have done. That record is on the NFL page: in testing on 2019–2026 our picks were right 68% of the time when they sided with Vegas's favorite and 44% when they went against it, and picks we call 50–55% are coin flips. The card also sets Vegas's point spread next to our margin. Over every game since 2002 our side of the spread won 51.5%, short of the 52.4% that breaks even, so a side close to the line is context only. Sides 3.5 or more points from it are the exception, and are marked as bets.

Injury report and What if?

A team's run game, pass game, run defense and pass defense are rated in percent of their normal yards per play. A player out lowers his units by what an absence at his position has cost teams in results (or what Vegas charges, where results are too noisy), times his snap share; a QB change goes through the model with the new QB's rating. Yards become points (0.07 a yard) and points become win chance (0.137 log-odds a point, from how Vegas turns spreads into odds). The final injury report's Out and Doubtful players reprice each game the morning of, or the day before, it.

Stuff+

A grade for every pitch from how it's thrown, never where it went or what happened: velocity, movement, spin and spin axis, release height, side and extension, arm angle, approach angle at the plate, and its difference in speed and movement from the pitcher's own fastball. A gradient-boosted model per pitch group (fastballs, breaking balls, off-speed) learns what pitches like it are worth in runs - with balls in play valued by their exit velocity and launch angle, not by where the fielders stood. Each pitcher is graded by a model that never saw him, so it can't simply remember his results. 100 is league average, every 10 points one standard deviation between pitchers, pulled toward 100 for small samples. It holds up: a pitcher's grade in odd games matches his grade in even games at r = 0.91 (his actual runs allowed, 0.24), and 2025 Stuff+ adds to what his 2025 results say about 2026.

Decision+

A grade for a hitter's swing-or-take choices. Every pitch he saw is placed by where it crossed his own strike zone, the count, the outs, the runners, the pitch type and the pitcher's hand. League models give, for that spot, how often hitters swing and what swinging and taking are each worth in runs - league-average results, so it's the decision being graded, not his contact. Each pitch scores what his choice was worth over the league's usual mix there. 100 is average, every 10 points one standard deviation between hitters. It's one of the steadiest things a hitter does (r = 0.75 from 2025 to 2026, more than walk rate's 0.61), it predicts next season's walk rate (r = 0.48) and adds to it, and it says nothing about strikeouts - whether he makes contact is a separate skill.

MLB game simulation

Every pitch is drawn from the stored Statcast pitches of the last two seasons. The pitcher picks a pitch as he does in that count against that side of the plate, at his speed for it. Its result - a ball, called strike, swinging strike, foul, ball in play or hit batter - is the league's rate for that pitch type in that count, times the pitcher's own rate for that pitch, the hitter's against everything he's seen (each measured against what the league would have done with the same pitches in the same counts, and, for small samples, started from what his Stuff+ or Decision+ says until a few hundred pitches of his own take over), the platoon edge and the times through the order. A ball in play becomes a groundout, flyout, lineout, single, double, triple or home run the same way, and the runners move as they did after that kind of play with those runners on and that many outs - double plays, sacrifice flies and taking the extra base included. Starters go about as many pitches as they have lately; relievers come in by role, the closer for a save. Between pitches runners steal, get caught or picked off and move up on wild pitches as often as they do in that base-out spot, and hitters are walked intentionally as often as managers call it there. Extra innings start with a runner on second. Run over many games it scores about 4.4 runs a team, strikes out 23% of hitters, walks 9.9% and steals 0.74 bases a game, against the league's 4.5, 22%, 10% and 0.74. "Simulate it 1,000 times" plays the matchup a thousand times: who wins how often, the spread of margins and the commonest scores, and each player's averages, next to our pick model's chance for the same game.

Game simulation

Every snap is drawn from the teams' own play-by-play this season: how often each throws on early downs, how its runs and completions are spread, and how often it's sacked, picked off or fumbles, pulled toward the league's with 150 plays of league average. Field position matters the way it does for the league: inside the 10 teams run more, complete fewer passes and gain less (real teams complete 49% there against 66% between the 20s), and on 4th and 2 there they go for it 61% of the time; pinned inside their own 10 they run more and rarely lose yards into a safety. A team's own red-zone numbers aren't used - they barely carry over from one half of a season to the next. Kickoffs, punts, field goals by distance and extra points use the league's last two seasons. The profiles carry style, not strength: each team's gains are scaled to the league's average, and the game is then tuned, from 300 seeded simulations, so the average simulated game lands on our pick's margin (moved by any What if? changes, at 0.137 log-odds a point) and our predicted total. Each team also has a good or bad day, and the two share part of it, in amounts fitted to real games (how). Unit settings move the yards where they belong - a pass defense at 80% gives up more through the air. Play calls follow how teams call them: short yardage mostly runs, and to the lead back (late in a close game too); a team ahead late runs the clock, throwing about as rarely as real ones; fourth downs go for it the way teams do now (4th and 1 near midfield, 4th and short when behind late); a team kneels only when kneeling on every down left runs out the clock, timeouts and the two-minute warning counted. Overtime gives both teams the ball. Penalties come at each team's rate a snap (pulled hard toward the league's - a team's flag rate barely carries over from one half of a season to the next) in the league's mix: false starts and holding walked back, pass interference spotted, roughing and other personal fouls added on with a first down. Kickoffs and punts are real ones from the last two seasons: a kickoff is a touchback, out of bounds, or caught at a spot and returned (a deep catch comes back farther), and a punt kicked from about where the offense is, then returned, fair caught, downed, out of bounds or a touchback - with return touchdowns as often as real ones. Onside kicks are left out (half of the real kickoff drives that start past midfield followed one); a team behind in the 4th quarter kicks onside, and recovers about 1 in 13. Each team gets three timeouts a half; it spends them as teams do - the defense to stop the clock late when it's within two scores, the offense when it's behind late or driving at the half - and the two-minute warning stops the clock. Each team's targets and carries go to its players by the share each has had in the games he was active for (mostly his last four, partly the whole season), so a player back from injury keeps the role he had; a player's catch rate and gains are his own next to his teammates', pulled toward theirs. Mark a player out in What if? and his share goes to the rest (a running back's carries to the other backs); swap the QB and the new one throws and takes the QB's runs. Offenses also lean a little toward what a defense gives up: against one that has been run on and gashed, a team runs a play or two more. Simulate it 1,000 times plays the same game a thousand times for how often each side wins, the spread of margins and totals, and each player's averages - how often he scores, and how often he reaches 100 yards.

College basketball ratings and the bracket

Every team gets an offense number and a defense number: points scored above, and allowed below, what an average Division I team would manage over 100 possessions (pace is taken out, so a fast team's big scores don't flatter it). All of them are solved at once across every game of the season, so each performance is judged against the opponent it came against and where it was played; home court is worth about 3.5 points. Each rating starts the season at 85% of last season's (a little higher for a team in the preseason AP top 25, which knows about transfers and departures we don't) and is held there with the weight of six games, so by January this season's games carry it. Weighting recent games more was tested and made the picks worse. We also set the picks against four seasons of sportsbook lines (22,940 games): the line was a little closer than we were on both the margin and the total, and taking our side of it won about half the time however far apart we were, so the college picks are not bets. These are what other sites call adjusted offensive and defensive efficiency; the Rankings tab's Deep dive view shows them as points per 100 possessions, with each team's chance of beating an average team on a neutral floor (the idea behind the "Barthag" number some sites publish).

A game: the two ratings' gap, over the pace the two teams figure to play at, is the expected margin, and games land about 11 points either side of it - that spread gives the win chance. Replayed week by week over 92,350 games since 2010, never using a game to predict itself, the favorite won 73% of the time and the percentages held up (teams we gave 70-80% won 75% of the time).

The bracket is those game odds played forward 20,000 times ("Play it out" shows one of those runs at a time, upsets and all; past tournaments have had about 20 upsets of our favorite, and our odds expect 19). On the 1,130 NCAA tournament games since 2009, picked before each tournament began, our favorite won 70%; where the data has seeds (2023–2026), ours won 186 of 247 games between different seeds and the better seed won 182. That is the ceiling worth knowing: a good rating is roughly as good as the seeds at picking winners, and its value is in the percentages and in the games the seeds get wrong. Before Selection Sunday the bracket shown is our own: each conference's best-rated team plus the best of the rest, seeded down the ratings. Against real brackets that stand-in gets about 34 of the 37 at-large teams right, and misses mostly on small-conference champions, who earn their spot by winning a conference tournament.

The Time Machine tab uses each team's end-of-season rating from every year since 2009. To put different years on one scale, each team is measured against the 50 best teams of its own season rather than the average team: the best teams have pulled away from the pack lately, and without that step the last two seasons would take most of the top spots. It shows how far a team stood above the good teams of its day - it can't know how a 2010 roster would handle today's game - and it is there for fun, not for picks.

NFL team units

Each unit's EPA per play, solved for every offense and defense at once so each is credited for the opponents it faced, with recent games weighted more and reaching back into last season. Shown on a 100 = league-average scale, 30 points per standard deviation.

Player props, age curves, prospects

Props: a player's production blended over his season, last 8 and last 4 games, against what his opponent allows his position. Age curves: nearest neighbors among historical seasons at the same age. Prospects: each source's ranks blended into one list. The Stats pages (WAVE, CAVE, WOPS, QB+ and the rest) describe; only the Picks pages predict.

Site terms

Beat the Streak

MLB's contest: pick a hitter each day; the streak grows when he gets a hit and resets when he doesn't. 57 in a row wins the grand prize - one more than Joe DiMaggio's record.

Injury report: before / after

An NFL pick logged Tuesday, before the final injury report (purple outline), or repriced with it the morning of, or the day before, the game.

Regular

A player on at least half his side's snaps over his last four active games. What if? lists anyone at 30%.

Average regular / average starter

In What if?, a typical regular at a position (or a typical starting QB) - for someone not listed, or not yet rated.

Unit %

In What if?, a unit's yards per play as a percent of its normal; a defense at 90% allows 10% more.

Hitting

WAVE

A hitter's batting average - hits per at-bat - blended over the full season and his last 81, 30 and 10 days, so recent games count more, and pulled toward the league average when he has few at-bats (see shrinkage). Walks don't count as at-bats, so they neither help nor hurt it. A later test found the recent windows chase streaks that are not real (what it found); a season-based version is being tested.

On the site: MLB Stats, and underneath every Beat the Streak pick.

Hit chance

His chance of at least one hit in a typical game of 3.5 at-bats at his WAVE: one minus the chance he misses every time, 1 - (1 - WAVE)^3.5. A .280 hitter gets about 68% (see the complement rule).

On the site: MLB Stats.

CAVE (Carried Average)

The share of a hitter's games with at least one hit. A 1-for-4 and a 4-for-4 count the same - it measures how steadily he hits, not how much.

On the site: MLB Stats.

OPS and WOPS

OPS is on-base percentage (how often he reaches base) plus slugging (total bases per at-bat), a quick measure of overall hitting. WOPS is his OPS blended over the same windows as WAVE.

On the site: MLB Stats.

vs. pitchers faced

A hitter's numbers next to what a league-average hitter would have done against the same pitchers. Two hitters can both hit .270; the one who did it against aces did more.

On the site: the MLB Stats hitter table's second view.

Approach

A hitter's games-with-a-hit rate times his hit chance - how Beat the Streak ranks the hitters who qualify (both above 70%).

On the site: MLB Picks.

Decision+

A grade for a hitter's swing-or-take choices: each pitch he saw is placed by where it crossed his strike zone, the count, outs, runners and pitch type, and scored by what his choice was worth over what the league usually does there. 100 is average; every 10 points is one standard deviation. It grades the decision, not the contact.

On the site: MLB Stats; it also starts the simulator's estimate for hitters with few pitches.

wOBA and xwOBA

wOBA weights each way of reaching base by the runs it's worth - a home run counts about twice a single, not four times. xwOBA is the wOBA a batted ball "should" have produced from its exit velocity and launch angle, whether or not a fielder made a great play.

On the site: Stuff+ values balls in play the same way, by exit velocity and launch angle.

Pitching

PAVE and PAVE+

PAVE is the batting average hitters manage against a pitcher, blended over the same windows as WAVE. PAVE+ compares it with the league: below 1 is tougher than average.

On the site: MLB Stats (today's starters, pitchers giving up the most hits).

Stuff+

A grade for how a pitch is thrown - velocity, movement, spin, release, approach angle and how it plays off the pitcher's fastball - never where it went or what happened. 100 is league average; every 10 points is one standard deviation between pitchers, so a 120 is about two above.

On the site: MLB Stats (Best stuff), and the simulator.

FIP (Fielding Independent Pitching)

An ERA-like number built only from what a pitcher controls without his fielders: home runs, walks and hit batters, and strikeouts - (13 HR + 3 (BB + HBP) - 2 K) / IP plus a constant that puts it on ERA's scale.

On the site: Age Curves' pitcher projections.

Platoon edge

Hitters do better against pitchers who throw from the other side - a lefty against a righty sees the ball longer. The simulator applies the league's measured edge to every pitch.

On the site: the MLB game simulation.

Times through the order

Hitters do better each time they see the same pitcher in a game. It's one reason starters come out around the third time through.

On the site: the MLB game simulation.

Teams and games

Run differential

Runs scored minus runs allowed. Over a season it says more about a team than its record, which close games and luck push around.

On the site: MLB Stats (team schedules and profiles).

Pythagorean strength

The winning percentage a team's runs (or points) scored and allowed predict: RS^x / (RS^x + RA^x). With baseball's exponent of 1.83, a team scoring 750 and allowing 650 "should" win about 56.5%, or 92 games. Bill James named it for its resemblance to the Pythagorean theorem.

On the site: both game-pick models and the team profiles.

Strength of schedule

How good a team's opponents have been. A 10-5 start against the league's best is worth more than one against its worst.

On the site: team profiles, and the pick models' schedule-adjusted ratings.

Park factor

How much a ballpark adds to or takes away from scoring compared with an average park - each park's runs a game over the league's. Coors Field's thin air is the famous high one.

On the site: team profiles, and the hit chances in Beat the Streak.

Overall rating and recent form

Our blend of a team's strength measures into one number (1.00 is average), and the same over its recent games.

On the site: MLB Stats (Teams).

Football

EPA (expected points added)

Every down, distance and spot on the field is worth some number of points on average to the team with the ball - 1st and 10 at the opponent's 20 is worth far more than at your own 20. A play's EPA is how much it changed that. A 3rd-and-8 completion for 6 yards gains yards but loses expected points.

On the site: NFL Stats and Picks.

Net EPA per play

A team's offensive EPA per play minus the EPA per play its defense allows - one number for how much better it is than its opponents, play by play.

On the site: NFL power rankings.

QB+

A quarterback's EPA per dropback over his last 16 games, pulled toward a replacement-level backup when he has few.

On the site: NFL game picks, What if? and the Stats tab.

Receiving+, Rushing+, Pass D+, Run D+

Team unit ratings from EPA per play, each credited for the opponents it faced. 100 is league average, higher is always stronger, and 30 points is one standard deviation.

On the site: NFL Stats (Team units).

Points per drive and turnover margin

Points a team scores per possession, and takeaways minus giveaways. Both feed the NFL pick model.

On the site: NFL game picks.

Snap share

The share of his side's plays a player was on the field for. A regular is on at least half over his last four active games.

On the site: What if? and Player Props.

Floor

The fewest a player has put up in a single game this season - his worst day.

On the site: NFL Player Props.

Payroll

Relative payroll

A team's payroll per game over the league's that season. 2.0 spent twice the average. Dividing by the league's lets 1995 and 2025 be compared without adjusting for inflation.

On the site: Payroll.

Payroll effectiveness

Wins over average divided by payroll per win over the league's. Above 1 bought more winning per dollar than the league did.

On the site: Payroll.

Expected wins

What the ten seasons whose relative payroll was closest won, plus the front office's recent edge - what a team "should" win for what it spent.

On the site: Payroll.

Front office score

How many wins a franchise beat its payroll's expectation by, averaged over its previous three seasons.

On the site: Payroll.

Spending change

A team's relative payroll minus its average over the previous three seasons - positive means it spent up.

On the site: Payroll.

Cash paid and cap spending

For NFL teams: the dollars actually paid to players that season (signing bonuses counted when paid), or what counted against the salary cap (bonuses spread over the contract).

On the site: NFL Payroll.

Betting

Moneyline

American odds on who wins. -150 means you risk $150 to win $100; +130 means $100 wins $130.

On the site: the "Vegas" number on every game pick.

Implied probability

The chance a price implies: -150 is 150 / 250 = 60%; +130 is 100 / 230 = 43.5%. Turning odds into a probability is converting odds.

On the site: every game pick's Vegas number.

Vig and de-vig

The two sides' implied chances add to more than 100% - 60% + 43.5% = 103.5% above. The extra is the sportsbook's cut, the vig. De-vigging scales both down to add to 100% (58% and 42%), the book's real view.

On the site: "Vegas" always means the de-vigged chance.

Closing line

The odds just before the game starts, after the market has absorbed all the news. Beating it is the hardest test of a pick model.

On the site: every MLB pick is graded against it.

Edge

Our chance minus the market's. We suggest a bet only when it's at least 5 points.

On the site: MLB Picks.

Units

A standard stake - 1% of a bankroll - so records compare across bettors. Sizes come from the Kelly rule.

On the site: MLB Picks' bet record.

1. Describing data

Before predicting anything, you summarize what happened.

Rates and denominators

The idea A rate is a count over the chances it had - and which chances you count changes what it means.

In sports Batting average divides hits by at-bats; on-base percentage divides by plate appearances, so walks count. A patient hitter looks better by the second.

On this site WAVE counts at-bats only. We tested both: replaying the 2026 season, the Beat the Streak picks hit more often using at-bats than every plate appearance.

Learn more →

Weighted averages

The idea An average where some numbers count more than others.

In sports Is a hitter's last week or his whole season the better guide? Some of both: recent games say more about his form now, the season says more about who he is.

On this site WAVE, CAVE and WOPS weight the full season and the last 81, 30 and 10 days, so a hot or cold stretch moves them without taking over.

Learn more →

Standard deviation

The idea How spread out a set of numbers is - roughly, how far a typical value sits from the average.

In sports Two teams both average 4.5 runs; one scores 4 or 5 almost every night, the other 0 or 9. Same average, very different standard deviation - and the steady one wins more close games.

On this site It's the unit for our "plus" grades: on Stuff+ and Decision+ every 10 points is one standard deviation, on the NFL unit ratings every 30.

Learn more →

Z-scores and "plus" scales

The idea A z-score is how many standard deviations a value is from the average: (value - average) / standard deviation. It puts numbers with different units on one scale.

In sports Is a 98 mph fastball or a 3,000 rpm curveball more special? Their units can't be compared; their z-scores can.

On this site Stuff+ is 100 + 10 × z. A 120 is two standard deviations above average - in a bell curve, about the top 2-3% of pitchers.

Learn more →

Percentiles

The idea The share of a group a value is better than. The 90th percentile beats 90%.

In sports Baseball Savant's red-and-blue bars are percentiles: a 99 in exit velocity hits the ball harder than 99% of hitters.

On this site The bars in Look up a player rank him among regular players, worst on the left to best on the right.

2. Small samples

Sports hand you small samples every day. Knowing how little they say is half of analytics.

Sample size and noise

The idea The fewer the chances, the more luck moves a rate.

In sports A true .250 hitter goes 4-for-5 or better about 1.6% of the time - rare in any one game, but with about 270 hitters starting every day, someone does it almost daily. Nobody thinks he became an .800 hitter.

On this site It's why every rate we show is blended or pulled toward the league, and why the picks pages show how many picks a record is from.

Learn more →

Regression to the mean

The idea An extreme result is usually part skill, part luck. The luck doesn't repeat, so the next result tends to land closer to average.

In sports The "Sports Illustrated cover jinx": players reach the cover after a career-best stretch, and the next stretch is usually worse - not a curse, just luck running out.

On this site Decision+ carries over from one season to the next at a correlation of 0.75, so a hitter 20 points above average is expected about 15 above next year. NFL penalty rates barely carry over from one half of a season to the next, so the simulator pulls every team's nearly all the way to the league's.

Learn more →

Shrinkage

The idea Estimate a rate as if the player started with some extra, average results already on his record. With few real results the average dominates; with many, his own do. (It's a simple form of Bayesian estimation.)

In sports Add 50 at-bats of a .245 league average to a hitter's line: a 4-for-5 start becomes (4 + 12.25) / (5 + 50) = .295, while 60-for-200 barely moves (.289).

On this site That's WAVE's exact recipe. Stuff+ adds 100 average pitches, Decision+ 300, NFL team rates 150 plays - and the simulator starts a pitcher or hitter with few pitches from what his Stuff+ or Decision+ says.

Learn more →

Confidence intervals

The idea A range of values the truth could plausibly be, given the data - wider when the sample is small.

In sports A pick model that's 7-for-10 might be a 70% model - or a 40% one that got lucky. The 95% interval for 7 of 10 runs from about 40% to 89%. At 70 of 100 it narrows to 60% to 78%.

On this site Our backtests report intervals, not just averages - see the bootstrap below.

3. Probability

The language every pick on this site is written in.

The complement rule

The idea The chance something happens at least once is one minus the chance it never does.

In sports A .280 hitter misses 72% of the time, so in 3.5 at-bats he misses every time about 0.72^3.5 = 32% of the time - and gets at least one hit 68%.

On this site That's hit chance.

Learn more →

Independence

The idea Two events are independent if one happening doesn't change the chance of the other. Then you can multiply their chances.

In sports Two hitters in the same game aren't fully independent: a dominant starter or a cold, windy night hurts them both, so they tend to go hitless together.

On this site Hit chance treats each at-bat as independent - close, but not exact, which is one reason the pick models' chances are checked against real results (see calibration).

Learn more →

Odds and log-odds

The idea Odds are the chance something happens over the chance it doesn't: 60% is 60/40 = 1.5. Log-odds, the logarithm of that, turn a 0-to-100% scale into one with no ends, where adding works.

In sports Each point of NFL spread is worth about 0.137 in log-odds: a 3-point favorite wins about 60%, a 7-point favorite about 72%.

On this site What if? and the NFL simulator turn point edges into win chances this way; a logistic regression works in log-odds throughout.

Learn more →

log5

The idea Bill James' rule for combining two rates measured against everyone into the rate for one matchup, each relative to the league.

In sports A .300 hitter against a pitcher who allows .220, in a .250 league: (.300 × .220 / .250) / (that + .700 × .780 / .750) = .266. Good hitter, tough pitcher - a bit above average.

On this site Beat the Streak's matchup between each hitter's WAVE and the starter's PAVE.

Learn more →

Expected value

The idea The average result of a bet if you could make it over and over: each outcome times its chance, added up.

In sports $100 on a +150 underdog you think wins 45%: 0.45 × $150 - 0.55 × $100 = +$12.50. You'll lose this bet more often than not, and it's still a good one.

On this site A bet is suggested only when our chance gives it a clear positive expected value against the market (an edge of 5 points or more).

Learn more →

4. Relationships and models

How one thing moves with another - and how to turn that into a prediction.

Correlation

The idea A number from -1 to 1 for how closely two things move together in a straight line. 0 is no straight-line relationship; it isn't proof that one causes the other.

In sports Split each pitcher's season into odd and even games. His Stuff+ in one half matches the other at 0.91; his runs allowed, only 0.24. Stuff is a skill you can measure quickly; runs allowed take far longer to mean much.

On this site That split-half test is how we checked Stuff+ (and Decision+, 0.75 season to season) before using either.

Learn more →

Linear regression

The idea The straight line that best fits one number from others. Its slope says how much the result moves per unit of the input.

In sports Across NFL games, every extra yard a team gains is worth about 0.07 points - so a 100-yard edge is about a touchdown.

On this site What if? uses that slope to turn yards into points.

Learn more →

Logistic regression

The idea Regression for yes-or-no outcomes: a weighted sum of inputs, in log-odds, turned into a chance between 0 and 100%.

In sports Win or lose: each input - point differential, the quarterback, turnovers - pushes the log-odds up or down by its weight.

On this site Half of the NFL game pick is a logistic regression on Pythagorean strength, EPA edges, turnover margin, points per drive and each starter's QB+ (the other half predicts the margin).

Learn more →

Gradient boosting

The idea Hundreds of small decision trees ("is velocity over 95? is the count 0-2?"), each built to fix the mistakes of the ones before. It finds curves and combinations a straight line misses.

In sports A 92 mph fastball can be great with lots of rise and ordinary without - the value of speed depends on movement. Trees catch interactions like that.

On this site Stuff+ is a boosted model per pitch group, and so is the Beat the Streak hit model.

Learn more →

Nearest neighbors

The idea Predict by finding the most similar past cases and seeing what happened to them. No equation - just comparisons.

In sports A 27-year-old with a .280 average: what did the 25 most similar 27-year-old seasons do the next year?

On this site Age Curves projects next season from exactly those 25 comparable seasons, with the range they spanned.

Learn more →

Overfitting

The idea A model that memorizes its data - noise included - instead of learning what carries over. It looks brilliant on the past and fails on the future.

In sports "Teams wearing gray on Tuesdays are 14-3" - a pattern, but not one that will hold.

On this site Each pitcher's Stuff+ comes from a model that never saw his pitches, so it can't remember his results; every model is judged only on games it wasn't trained on (see walk-forward testing).

5. Testing ideas

How we decide whether something is real - and whether it's worth using.

Hypothesis tests and p-values

The idea Assume there's no effect, then ask how likely data this extreme would be. That's the p-value. A small one (under 0.05, by convention) says "probably not just chance."

In sports A coin-flip picker goes 60-for-100 about 3% of the time - so a 60% record over 100 picks is decent evidence of skill. Over 10 picks (6-4), it's no evidence at all.

On this site Across 48,804 hitter-games, facing a pitcher with better Stuff+ meant fewer hits at p = 0.00000005 - about as sure as statistics gets.

Learn more →

Significant isn't the same as useful

The idea A p-value says an effect is real, not that it's big - or that it adds anything to what you already know.

In sports Teams do score slightly more in warm weather - but if you already know the park and the pitchers, temperature may add almost nothing.

On this site That Stuff+ effect, real as it was, didn't improve the hit model: it already knew how hittable each pitcher had been, and its accuracy didn't measurably change. So it isn't in the model. (Stuff+ still helps the simulator with pitchers it has seen little of.)

Learn more →

Backtests and holdouts

The idea Test a model on past games it never learned from, scored exactly as it would have been live. A holdout is a final stretch kept untouched until the very end.

In sports It's a mock draft graded against how the players actually turned out.

On this site No change to a pick model ships without one, and the README keeps the results - the ones that failed too.

Walk-forward testing

The idea Train on everything before a date, predict the next stretch, step forward and repeat - never using the future to predict the past.

In sports Predicting May's games with a model trained through April, then June's with one trained through May - the way you'd really have had to do it.

On this site How every pick model is chosen and checked, by month or by week.

Data leakage

The idea When a model sees information it wouldn't have had at prediction time. Its backtest looks great; live, it falls apart.

In sports Rating a starter with a season ERA that includes the very game you're predicting.

On this site Every feature is computed through the day before the game - a pitcher's Stuff+ in a backtest uses only pitches he'd already thrown.

The bootstrap

The idea To see how much a result could vary, resample your own data - draw from it, with replacement, a thousand times - and recompute it each time. The spread is your uncertainty.

In sports Rebuild a season by drawing 162 games at random from the real ones, a thousand times, and see how much the team's record swings.

On this site Our model comparisons report a bootstrapped 95% interval for the difference - for the Stuff+ test, -0.0007 to +0.0005 in Brier score, which straddles zero: no measurable change.

6. Judging forecasts

A 60% pick that loses wasn't necessarily wrong. These tools grade probabilities fairly.

Brier score

The idea Score each forecast by how far it was from what happened, counting a win as 1 and a loss as 0: (forecast - result)². Squaring makes a big miss cost much more than a small one. Average over every game; lower is better, and 0 is perfect.

In sports You give a team a 70% chance. If it wins, the result is 1: (0.70 - 1)² = 0.09. If it loses, the result is 0: (0.70 - 0)² = 0.49. Saying 50% scores (0.50 - 1)² or (0.50 - 0)² = 0.25 either way, so 0.25 is the coin-flip baseline. If your 70% teams really win 7 in 10, you average 0.7 × 0.09 + 0.3 × 0.49 = 0.21 - better than the coin flip.

On this site Baseball games are close to coin flips - our MLB game models land around 0.244, a real but modest edge on 0.25. It's the score we choose models by.

Learn more →

Log loss

The idea Like Brier, but a confident miss costs far more: saying 99% and being wrong is a disaster.

In sports "There's no way they lose" - log loss is the score that makes that expensive.

On this site Reported next to Brier for every pick model.

Learn more →

Calibration

The idea Forecasts are calibrated when they mean what they say: the games called 60% are won about 60% of the time.

In sports A weather forecaster's 30% days should rain about 3 times in 10. A picker whose "locks" win 55% isn't calibrated.

On this site The MLB Picks chart "When we were more sure, were we right more often?" is a calibration check, and every model's chances are adjusted after training so they line up.

AUC

The idea How well a model ranks: the chance it puts a random "yes" above a random "no". 0.5 is a coin flip, 1.0 is perfect. It ignores whether the percentages themselves are right.

In sports Does the hitter we ranked higher get a hit more often than the one we ranked lower?

On this site The Beat the Streak hit model's is about 0.60. That sounds low - but a single game is mostly luck, which is exactly why a long streak is so hard.

The market as a benchmark

The idea Compare a model with the best forecast already out there, not with nothing.

In sports Betting markets pool thousands of bettors' money and information; their closing lines are hard to beat.

On this site Every pick sits next to the de-vigged Vegas chance, and when the NFL model disagrees with Vegas sharply, it defers - Vegas has been right more often there.

7. Simulation

When the math gets too tangled to solve, play it out.

Monte Carlo simulation

The idea Answer "how often?" by playing a random process many times and counting. The more runs, the smaller the error.

In sports Play a matchup 1,000 times; if one team wins 600, its chance is about 60% - give or take 3 points, since 2 × √(0.6 × 0.4 / 1,000) ≈ 0.03.

On this site "Simulate it 1,000 times" in both game simulators: win chances, the spread of scores and each player's chances of a hit, a homer or 100 yards.

Learn more →

Markov chains and base-out states

The idea A process that moves between states, where what happens next depends only on where you are now, not how you got there.

In sports Baseball has 24 base-out states (8 ways to fill the bases, 3 out counts). A single with a runner on first and one out leads to first-and-second 72% of the time and first-and-third 25%.

On this site The MLB simulator moves runners exactly this way, with each play's real outcomes in each state; football's equivalent is down, distance and field position.

Checking a simulation against reality

The idea A simulation is only as good as its match to real games, so measure it.

In sports If simulated teams score 5.5 runs a game, something's wrong - real ones score about 4.5.

On this site Over 2,000 simulated games, MLB teams score 4.44 runs, strike out 22.9% and steal 0.74 bases a game, against the league's 4.46, 22.2% and 0.74.

8. Making decisions

A good forecast still needs a good decision rule.

The Kelly rule

The idea How much of a bankroll to stake on a bet with an edge: enough to grow it fastest over time, never so much that a losing run ruins you. Stake more when the edge is bigger.

In sports At even money with a 55% chance, Kelly says 10% of the bankroll. Since our 55% is itself an estimate, we bet half that.

On this site Suggested bets use half-Kelly in units, capped at 2 units a game and 5 a day.

The idea When to stop and lock in what you have versus keep going, solved by working backward from the end (dynamic programming).

In sports In Beat the Streak, a long streak is worth protecting: some days the best pick isn't good enough to risk it, and sitting out keeps the streak alive.

9. Newer methods

Statistics from the last several years that have barely reached sports. Each one here was tried on our own data, and what it found is reported whether or not it flattered us.

Evidence you can check every week (anytime-valid testing)

The idea An ordinary significance test is only honest if you look once, at a sample size fixed in advance. Look every week and stop when it looks good, and a coin flip passes sooner or later. The fix, worked out over the last few years, measures evidence as money: a doubter starts with 1 unit and stakes a slice of it on every bet in the series, in order, betting that the claim of "no edge" is wrong. If there truly is no edge, that bankroll shrinks, and the chance it ever reaches 20 is at most 1 in 20 - at any moment, with no sample size named in advance. So the bankroll is the evidence, and it can be read as often as you like.

In sports A tout who is 60% over his last 100 picks may just be showing you the 100 that looked best. The bankroll cannot be gamed that way: every bet since the start counts, and a hot stretch that fades takes the bankroll back down with it.

On this site Every kind of bet we post has a row under How strong is the evidence? on the NFL Picks tab. What it found: our spread bets, replayed over every season since 2002, turn 1 unit into about 20 - right at the bar, not far past it - after peaking near 65 in late 2021, and the bankroll sat below 1 until 2014. The over/under leans (58.6% of 174) reach only about 3: fewer than 200 bets cannot carry more, however good the percentage looks. Our winner in every game at the closing moneyline shrinks the bankroll to almost nothing, which is why winners are shown as picks, not bets. Next to each bankroll is the range of win rates the results have not ruled out - also safe to read every week, so it is wider than a usual margin of error (spread bets: 52.4% to 61.8%, and break-even is 52.4%) - and a fade alarm that watches for the win rate dropping under break-even starting at any point. One limit no method removes: a rule chosen by looking at past results is flattered by those results, so the replay rows are the most the evidence could be. The bets posted here before the games are the clean test, and they have only just begun.

On the site: NFL Picks, under the track record. Learn more →

Ranges that hit as often as they say (conformal prediction)

The idea A range is only useful if "4 times in 5" means 4 times in 5. The usual way to make one assumes the misses follow a bell curve. Conformal prediction assumes no shape at all: it takes the model's own past misses - on games it had not seen when it predicted them - and reads the range straight off them. If 80% of past misses fell between 19 under and 31 over, the next range is the prediction minus 19 to plus 31. As long as the next game is like the past ones, the stated hit rate is guaranteed for any model, good or bad; a worse model just gets wider ranges.

In sports A receiver's yards are nothing like a bell curve: most games land near his usual, a few go far over, none go below zero. A range built on a bell curve is too generous underneath and too stingy on top. And one width for everyone fails too - a 90-yard projection misses by far more than a 15-yard one.

On this site Three places. The point total on each NFL pick card keeps its half-the-time range and adds a 4-times-in-5 one; the margin gets both. Tested season by season on games after the ones the ranges were built from, the 4-in-5 total range caught 80.1% of 1,871 games and the margin's 80.5% of 5,581. (The check also confirmed the old hand-set "plus or minus 9" for the total: the misses say 8.9.) And every player projection on the Player Props tab now has a likely range, built week by week from earlier weeks only. Here the plain version failed in an instructive way: one range for all players caught 79% overall but 92% of the smallest third of receiving-yard projections and 59% of the largest. Keeping the misses of small and large projections apart, and sizing them to the projection, brought every third to 79-81%. The same past misses also give the chance a player clears any number, so each card has a box for the sportsbook's line. Against lines set at a player's own average, that chance was honest across 20,855 player-games: when it said 27% it happened 27% of the time; 64%, 64%; 80%, 79%. One caution: a sportsbook's line already knows the matchup and the injury report, and we have no past prop lines to test against, so against a real line be less sure than the number says.

On the site: NFL Picks (each game's card) and Player Props. Learn more →

Fitting a simulator to reality (simulation-based inference)

The idea A simulator has settings that no formula connects to the data: there is no equation for "how spread out are final scores" in terms of "how much does a team's day vary". The newer answer is to stop looking for the formula. Draw hundreds of candidate settings, run the simulator at each, and keep the ones whose simulated games look like the real ones, each weighted by how close it came. What comes out is not a single best guess but the whole range of settings the data cannot tell apart. The idea is taking off in physics and biology, where simulators are common and formulas are not.

In sports Almost nobody has a play-by-play simulator to fit. We do, and it had a measurable flaw: built from plays alone, its games were too alike. Final margins landed 12.3 points either side of the target and totals 12.2, where real games land 12.8 and 13.1 around the closing lines. Teams have good days and bad days that plays drawn one at a time do not capture.

On this site We gave the football simulator two new settings - how much each offense's day varies, and how much of a day both teams share (weather, officials, pace) - and fitted them to 2,127 real games. Each of 400 candidate settings played 12,000 simulated games; the data settled on a team's day varying by about 8% (somewhere from 5% to 10%) and a shared day of about 4% (1% to 7%). With those, a fresh run matches real games on the spread of margins (12.7 against 12.8) and totals (13.2 against 13.1), and on numbers the fit never saw: totals within 9 points of the line 52.2% against a real 52.8% (it was 55.4%), and games of 58 points or more 19.9% against 19.8% (it was 17.8%). It also showed what the simulator still gets wrong, which no setting of these could fix: real games finish within a field goal 24.7% of the time and simulated ones 19%. The same check found the cause of a mismatch on the page: the simulation was aiming at a margin from our points model, whose margins run much closer together than games do, so a game our pick had at 88% simulated at 72%. It now aims at the pick's own margin; across this week's games the simulated win rate is within 4 points of the pick in every one.

On the site: NFL Picks, Simulate this game. Learn more →

Spotting a real change (online changepoint detection)

The idea Every "recent form" number blends fixed windows: the last four games, the last 30 days. A fixed window is wrong both ways. When nothing has changed it throws away good data to chase noise; when something really changes it takes the window's whole length to catch up. Changepoint detection has no window. After every game it keeps a probability for each answer to "how many games ago did his current level begin?" - and its estimate uses all of his history when nothing has changed, only the games since the break when one is clear, and an honest mix while it is unsure.

In sports A back who loses the starting job, a pitcher whose fastball drops two miles an hour, a hitter on a tear: three stories that sound alike. The method can tell which of them are real.

On this site We tried it on all three, choosing its settings on earlier seasons and scoring on later ones, and got three different answers. A pitcher's fastball: real changes, and the method wins. Predicting his next game's speed it beat his season average and every fixed window, most in the one game in eight where it flagged a change (a miss of 0.56 mph against 0.58 for his last five games and 0.64 for his season). Who gets the ball in football: roles do change, but the simple blend the simulator already uses (mostly a player's last four active games) predicts the next game's carries and targets just as well: the blend put 21.6% of a game's carries with the wrong player and the new method 21.8%, and they tied on targets. We kept the blend. A hitter's hot and cold streaks: nothing. The method saw a likely change before none of 39,443 hitter-games, and its estimate was simply his season average. That exposed a weakness in WAVE, which leans on a hitter's last 10 and 30 days: when WAVE had a hitter 67 points above his season, he went on to hit 3 points under it, and WAVE predicted at-bats worse than giving every hitter the league average. WAVE is one input among many in our hit model, so the picks are not that far off - but it is the wrong input, and reworking it is next.

Learn more →

Reading an at-bat like a sentence (sequence models)

The idea The models behind chatbots read words in order and learn what tends to come next. The same kind of model can read anything that comes in order - here, the pitches of an at-bat. Give it every earlier pitch and how each ended, ask it what the next one will do, and compare it with the same model shown only the pitch itself. The gap between the two is exactly what the sequence is worth.

In sports Pitchers and catchers talk about sequencing constantly: a fastball up to set up the curve, never the same pitch three times. Almost every public pitching number, ours included, ignores it and grades each pitch on its own.

On this site We trained the models on 970,546 pitches (2025 through May 2026) and scored them on 337,891 pitches from July to October 2026 that they had never seen. Every pitch ends one of five ways - ball, called strike, whiff, foul, in play - and each model gives the chance of each. The sequence is real and small: reading the at-bat improved the predictions by 1.6% of what the pitch itself tells you (its type, speed, movement, location, the count and who is involved). It is nothing on the first pitch and grows with every pitch after. Two thirds of it comes from what was thrown before, one third from how the hitter handled it. The patterns run against the old advice to change speeds: a hitter swings and misses more than the pitch alone predicts when a pitcher repeats a kind of pitch (a fastball after a fastball, 20.3% of swings against 19.1% expected; a breaking ball after another, 32.9% against 32.1%) and less when he switches (a breaking ball or changeup right after a fastball, 29.4% against 31.4%). After a swing and miss the next swing misses more; after a foul, less. Read that with care: pitchers choose, and they go back to a pitch when the hitter has looked bad on it, so this describes what happens, not what to call. It is not in our picks or the simulator yet. At 1.6% it would move little, and the first thing it would improve is the simulator's pitch by pitch outcomes deep in an at-bat.

Baseball, pitch by pitch

Built every day from the last two seasons of Statcast - every pitch thrown, with its type, speed, count, location and result.

Which pitch he throws

Each pitcher throws his own mix - every pitch he uses at least 3% of the time - and the mix changes with the count and the hitter's side, as his really does: first pitch, ahead in the count, even, or behind. A pitcher who leans on his slider when ahead of a right-handed hitter does so here too. Its speed comes from his own average and spread for that pitch.

What the pitch does

Every pitch ends one of six ways: ball, called strike, swinging strike, foul, hit by pitch, or put in play. We start from how often each happens league-wide for that pitch type in that count - a 3-0 fastball is a ball far more often than an 0-2 slider. Then we adjust:

• the pitcher's own rates for that pitch, against what the league would have done with the same pitches in the same counts - so a pitcher isn't credited or blamed just for being ahead in counts;

• the hitter's rates against everything he's seen, measured the same way;

• the platoon edge (same side or opposite) and the times through the order (hitters do better the third time they see a pitcher).

The adjustments multiply. Small samples are pulled toward the league (shrinkage: 250 pitches for a pitcher, 400 for a hitter), and a pitcher or hitter with few pitches starts from what his Stuff+ or Decision+ says, until enough of his own take over (600 of a pitch type for a pitcher, 1,500 pitches for a hitter). A last set of league-wide multipliers, each within half a percent of 1, makes the whole product add up to what really happened between these players.

What happens when it's hit

A ball in play becomes a groundout, flyout, lineout, single, double, triple or home run, the same way: the league's rates, times the pitcher's (200 balls in play of pull toward average) and the hitter's (150).

How the runners move

Not by rule but by record. For every kind of play, every arrangement of runners and every number of outs, we counted where the runners actually ended up - so a single with a runner on first and one out leaves first and second 72% of the time and first and third 25%, double plays and sacrifice flies happen as often as they really do, and a fast runner taking the extra base is in the averages. Between pitches, runners steal, get caught or picked off, and move up on wild pitches as often as they do in that spot; when every runner moves up a base it's a steal 75% of the time, otherwise a wild pitch. Hitters are walked intentionally as often as managers do it with first base open, more late in close games.

The manager's decisions

Starters get a pitch count drawn around their own recent average, between 45 and 115; they come out when they reach it (a few pitches early between innings, a few late mid-inning) or when an inning gets away from them (5 runs, or 4 past 70 pitches). Relievers come in by role: the closer in the 9th or later when his team is tied or ahead by up to 3, the setup man in a close 8th, anyone else as often as he really pitches - so the whole bullpen gets used, not just its best. A reliever usually gets his inning, sometimes two. Lineups default to each team's regulars in their usual spots; you can pick your own. Extra innings start with a runner on second, as in the majors.

Football, snap by snap

Built every week from this season's play-by-play for each team, and the last two seasons for the league (kickoffs, punts, field goals, penalties).

Style, not strength

Each offense has its own profile: how often it throws on early downs in a close game, how often it's sacked, completes a pass, throws an interception or fumbles, and the whole spread of its runs and completions (not just the average - a team that lives on 4-yard runs and one that breaks 40-yarders look different). Small samples are pulled toward the league with 150 plays. Then each team's gains are scaled to the league's average, so the profile carries how a team plays but not how good it is.

Where strength comes from

Strength is set to agree with our own pick. Before you watch, 300 practice games tune two dials - a yards edge for the stronger team and an overall scale - until the average simulated game lands on the What if? panel's win chance (turned into a point margin: each point is worth about 0.137 in log-odds) and on our predicted total. Mark a player out or change a unit's percent and the yards move where they belong: a pass defense at 80% gives up more through the air, not on the ground.

What play is called

A pass or a run, at the team's own early-down pass rate, adjusted the way teams adjust:

• 3rd and 4th down by distance: 25% passes on 3rd and 1, 40% on 3rd and 2, 65% on 3rd and 3-4, 85% when longer; 2nd and long throws more, 2nd and short less;

• ahead late (the last four minutes), teams run the clock: 6% passes on first down, 20% on second, 25% or 55% on a short or long third;

• behind by two scores from the third quarter on, more passes; in the hurry-up, more again;

• field position: inside the opponent's 10 teams pass 20% less than their downs would suggest, and pinned inside their own 10, 12% less.

Who gets the ball

Targets and carries go to each team's players by the share each has had in the games he was active for (mostly his last four, partly the season), so a player back from missed games keeps his role. In short yardage, at the goal line and late in close games, the lead back gets the ball more - shares are squared, so a back with 83% of the carries gets about 98%. Mark a player out and his share goes to the rest; swap the quarterback and the new one throws and takes the quarterback's runs.

What the play gains

A pass is first a sack or not, at the team's sack rate; then an interception or not; then complete or not, at the team's completion rate times the target's own catch rate. A completion or a run gains yards drawn from the team's own spread of gains, times the strength scale, times the player's own yards per catch or carry next to his teammates'. Near the goal line, completions drop (49% inside the 10 against 66% between the 20s) and gains shrink; pinned deep, losses shrink, so safeties are as rare as real ones. On 3rd or 4th and short, a run is stuffed 12% of the time on top of its draw - real teams convert 73% of them; simulated ones, 72%.

Fourth down and kicks

Fourth downs go for it the way teams decide now: 4th and 1 from your own 30 on; 4th and 2 from midfield and 4th and 3 from the opponent's 45 when a field goal is out of reach; inside the opponent's 10 by yards to go (4th and 2: 61%); behind late, more aggressively; otherwise a field goal when it's makeable (the league's make rate by distance) or a punt. Kickoffs and punts are real ones from the last two seasons: where the ball was caught and how far it came back are drawn together, as one real return, so a deep catch comes back farther, and return touchdowns happen about as often as real ones. A team behind late kicks onside and recovers about 1 in 13.

The clock and the referees

Plays in bounds take 40 seconds, incompletions 6. Each team has three timeouts a half and spends them as teams do - the defense to stop the clock late, the offense when behind or driving at the half - and the two-minute warning stops the clock. A team kneels only when kneeling on every remaining down actually ends the game. Penalties come at each team's rate (pulled hard toward the league's, since a team's flag rate barely carries over from one half of a season to the next) in the league's mix of fouls, enforced by the rulebook: false starts and holding walked back, pass interference spotted, personal fouls added on with an automatic first down.

1,000 games at once

What it's for

One simulated game is one story; a thousand are a forecast. "Simulate it 1,000 times" plays the same matchup with different dice each time (Monte Carlo) and reports how often each team wins, the spread of margins and totals, the commonest final scores, and each player's averages - his chance of a hit, a homer, a touchdown or 100 yards.

How much to trust it

A thousand games pins a win share down to about 3 points either way. The football simulator is tuned to our pick, so it adds the shape of the game - who scores, how it's won - more than a new chance. The baseball simulator isn't tuned to the pick: it builds the game from the players alone, so its win share is an independent second opinion, shown next to our pick model's.
